## Introduction: Crime, Criminal Liability and the Principle of Legality

An introduction to the purposes of criminal law, the elements of criminal liability, overcriminalisation and Article 7 ECHR, focusing on England & Wales and Northern Ireland.

## Overview

Criminal law identifies conduct that attracts criminal liability, establishes the conditions for conviction and authorises punishment. It also limits the state’s power to prosecute and punish: criminal liability must rest on an identifiable legal rule, not merely on official disapproval of someone’s behaviour. This requirement is central to the principle of **legality**. (European Convention on Human Rights, art 7.)[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)]

These notes focus on England & Wales and Northern Ireland. Shared principles provide a useful foundation, but the applicable legislation must always be checked for the particular jurisdiction. For example, Northern Ireland has its own statutory framework for criminal attempts and conspiracy. (Criminal Attempts and Conspiracy (Northern Ireland) Order 1983, SI 1983/1120 (NI 13).)[[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1983/1120)]

## Key Concepts and Definitions

### What is a crime?

A useful starting definition is conduct—an act or, where the law recognises a duty to act, an omission—that constitutes a criminal offence and attracts a legally authorised criminal sanction.

A crime does not have to be created by an Act of Parliament. The law recognised by Article 7 includes legislation and case law; an offence need not be placed on a statutory footing to satisfy the Convention. (European Court of Human Rights, *Guide on Article 7*, paras 8–10.)[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)]

Different approaches ask different questions about crime:


| Approach | Central question | Explanation |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Legal | What does the applicable criminal law prohibit? | Identifies offences through recognised legal sources, including legislation and common law. |
| Religious | What conduct violates divine law? | Evaluates conduct through religious obligations; a religious wrong is not automatically a criminal offence. |
| Social constructionist | How does behaviour come to be labelled criminal? | Examines the influence of social norms, culture, institutions and power on criminalisation. |
| Social-harm approach | What conduct seriously harms individuals or society? | Evaluates harmful behaviour, including conduct that may fall outside existing criminal offences. |


These approaches should be kept distinct. The legal approach identifies the rules a court applies; the others help evaluate why particular conduct is criminalised and whether the boundaries of criminal law are justified.

### Mala in se and mala prohibita


| Term | Meaning | Illustrative example |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Mala in se | Acts regarded as wrongful in themselves, independently of their legal prohibition. | Deliberate unjustified killing. |
| Mala prohibita | Acts regarded as wrongful because the law prohibits them, rather than because they are necessarily inherently immoral. | Breaching a technical licensing requirement. |


The distinction is an analytical aid, not a complete test of criminal liability. Whether conduct constitutes an offence depends on the applicable law, rather than solely on its perceived immorality. Article 7 requires a legal basis for conviction and punishment.[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)]

## Purposes of Criminal Law and Punishment

### The broader aims of criminal law

Criminal law can pursue several connected objectives:

- Maintaining public order and security.
- Protecting individuals, property and wider public interests.
- Expressing society’s condemnation of serious wrongdoing.
- Reducing offending through deterrence and prevention.
- Supporting rehabilitation and reparation.

The purposes of criminalisation and the purposes of sentencing are related but distinct. Criminalisation asks whether conduct should be an offence; sentencing asks what response is appropriate after an offender has been convicted.

### Purposes of sentencing

For adult offenders in England & Wales, the Sentencing Act 2020 identifies five purposes to which courts must have regard, subject to statutory exceptions. (Sentencing Act 2020, s 57.)[[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2020/17/section/57)]


| Purpose | Explanation |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Punishment | Imposing a penalty that expresses condemnation of the wrongdoing. |
| Reduction of crime | Preventing further offending, including through deterrence. |
| Reform and rehabilitation | Helping offenders change their behaviour and reintegrate into society. |
| Protection of the public | Protecting people, including victims, from further offending. |
| Reparation | Requiring offenders to make amends to those affected by their offences. |


Section 57 is an England & Wales provision, not a UK-wide sentencing code. Its list is useful for understanding sentencing objectives, but it should not be presented as the governing Northern Ireland provision.[[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2020/17/section/57)]

Punishment also requires justification. Fairness and proportionality ask whether the sanction is appropriate to the wrongdoing and the offender’s responsibility. Legality imposes a separate constraint: even a punishment thought morally deserved cannot be imposed without the necessary legal basis.[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)]

## Legal Framework: The Components of Criminal Liability

### Actus reus

*Actus reus* refers to the external elements of an offence. It is broader than the defendant’s physical movement and may include:

- Conduct: what the defendant did.
- An omission: a failure to act where a legally recognised duty exists.
- Circumstances: facts that must exist for the offence to be committed.
- A prohibited result and the necessary causal connection between the defendant’s conduct and that result.

For example, a result offence may require proof not merely that the defendant acted, but that their conduct caused the legally prohibited consequence. An omission does not normally create liability simply because intervening would have been morally desirable; a relevant legal duty must exist.[[getcaselaw](https://www.getcaselaw.com/guides/mens-rea-actus-reus)]

### Mens rea

*Mens rea* refers to the mental or fault elements required by the particular offence. It does not mean intention alone.

Depending on the offence, the prosecution may need to establish intention, knowledge, belief or recklessness. Some offences use negligence-based standards of fault. The precise requirement must be identified from the offence’s definition.[[getcaselaw](https://www.getcaselaw.com/guides/mens-rea-actus-reus)]

Mens rea is also distinct from motive:


| Concept | Question |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Mens rea | Did the defendant possess the legally required mental state? |
| Motive | Why did the defendant act? |


### Coincidence and strict liability

Ordinarily, the required actus reus and mens rea must coincide. However, the structure of liability varies between offences.

Strict liability means that mens rea need not be proved for at least one element of the offence. It does not mean that the prosecution can dispense with proving the offence’s remaining elements.[[getcaselaw](https://www.getcaselaw.com/guides/mens-rea-actus-reus)]

### Defences

A defence may prevent liability even where the prosecution establishes the offence’s elements.

Defences are broader than legal justifications. Some concern justification, such as lawful self-defence; others concern an excuse or a distinct basis for denying criminal responsibility. Their availability and requirements depend on the offence and the applicable law.

A useful introductory structure is:

> Identify the offence, establish its external and fault elements, and then consider any available defence.

This is a working framework rather than a universal formula: strict-liability offences and particular statutory schemes require separate attention.[[getcaselaw](https://www.getcaselaw.com/guides/mens-rea-actus-reus)]

### Inchoate offences

Inchoate offences criminalise conduct directed towards offending without requiring completion of the intended substantive offence.

They are not limited to attempts. Conspiracy is another important example.

In Northern Ireland:

- Attempt generally requires an intention to commit an offence and an act that is more than merely preparatory.
- Statutory conspiracy generally concerns an agreement to pursue a course of conduct that would necessarily involve an offence if carried out in accordance with the parties’ intentions, subject to the statutory provisions and exceptions.

(Criminal Attempts and Conspiracy (Northern Ireland) Order 1983, arts 3 and 9.)[[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1983/1120)]

These offences have their own elements. An attempt is therefore not simply a completed offence with an unsuccessful result.

### General principles and specific offences

General principles explain matters such as fault, causation and defences. Specific offences supply the particular requirements that must be proved.

The correct analytical order is therefore to identify the offence first, rather than assuming that every offence requires the same conduct, mental state and result.[[getcaselaw](https://www.getcaselaw.com/guides/mens-rea-actus-reus)]

## The Principle of Legality

### Nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege

The maxim *nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege* means that there can be no crime and no punishment without law.

Its central purpose is to protect people against arbitrary prosecution, conviction and punishment. It requires more than the existence of a rule somewhere: the legal basis must satisfy requirements of accessibility and reasonable foreseeability. (European Court of Human Rights, *Guide on Article 7*, paras 1–2 and 8.)[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)]

### Article 7(1) ECHR

Article 7(1) provides:

> No one shall be held guilty of any criminal offence on account of any act or omission which did not constitute a criminal offence under national or international law at the time when it was committed. Nor shall a heavier penalty be imposed than the one that was applicable at the time the criminal offence was committed.

(European Convention on Human Rights, art 7(1); Human Rights Act 1998, sch 1.)[[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1998/42/schedule/1)]

The provision protects against both retrospective criminalisation and the retrospective imposition of a heavier penalty.

Article 7 is given domestic effect through the Human Rights Act 1998. Section 6 generally makes it unlawful for public authorities to act incompatibly with Convention rights, subject to the Act’s exceptions.[[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1998/42/section/6)]

### Accessibility, foreseeability and limits on interpretation


| Requirement | Meaning |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accessibility | The relevant legal rules must be available to those affected. |
| Reasonable foreseeability | A person must be able to understand, with appropriate legal advice where necessary, what conduct attracts criminal liability. |
| Non-retroactivity | Conduct cannot retrospectively be made criminal to the defendant’s disadvantage. |
| Limits on expansive interpretation | Courts must not extend criminal liability to the defendant’s disadvantage through impermissible analogy. |
| Legally authorised punishment | The penalty must have a legal basis and cannot exceed the applicable legal limits. |


The law does not need to anticipate every possible factual situation. Judicial clarification is permissible where the development is consistent with the essence of the offence and was reasonably foreseeable. Legality therefore limits judicial development without freezing criminal law permanently.[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)][[hudoc.echr.coe](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=002-9450)]

### Historical contrast: Nazi Germany’s 1935 amendment

On 28 June 1935, Nazi Germany amended section 2 of its Penal Code to permit punishment not only for acts expressly prohibited by law, but also for acts considered deserving of punishment according to the fundamental conceptions of penal law and “sound popular feeling”.

Where no penal provision directly covered the conduct, the amendment authorised use of the provision considered most closely applicable. (Law to Change the Penal Code, 28 June 1935, art 1.)[[avalon.law.yale](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/imt/1962-ps.asp)]

The amendment illustrates the danger of punishment by analogy: the state could punish conduct without identifying a pre-existing offence that actually covered it.

### Article 7(2) and Nuremberg

Article 7(2) provides:

> This Article shall not prejudice the trial and punishment of any person for any act or omission which, at the time when it was committed, was criminal according to the general principles of law recognised by civilised nations.

(European Convention on Human Rights, art 7(2).)[[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1998/42/schedule/1)]

The crucial words are “at the time when it was committed”. Article 7(2) does not create a general power to punish conduct merely because a later court considers it immoral. The European Court treats it as a contextual clarification of Article 7(1), rather than a general exception permitting retrospective criminalisation.[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)]

The Nuremberg prosecutions were established under the 1945 London Agreement and Charter of the International Military Tribunal. Article 7(2) did not establish that tribunal or supply its original jurisdiction; it reflects the Convention’s historical concern not to undermine accountability for the crimes prosecuted after the Second World War.[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)][[ihl-databases.icrc](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/nuremberg-tribunal-charter-1945)]

## Case Law

### S.W. v United Kingdom (1995)

Citation and court: *S.W. v United Kingdom*, App no 20166/92 (ECtHR, 22 November 1995).

Facts: A man convicted of raping his wife argued that rejection of the historic marital immunity amounted to retrospective criminalisation.

Principle and outcome: The European Court found no violation of Article 7. The rejection of marital immunity continued a reasonably foreseeable development of the law and was consistent with the essence of rape.[[hudoc.echr.coe](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=002-9450)][[hudoc.echr.coe](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=001-57965)]

Weight and significance: This is a leading Strasbourg authority on permissible judicial development of criminal law. UK courts must take relevant Strasbourg decisions into account under section 2 of the Human Rights Act 1998; they are not binding domestic precedents in the same way as a binding decision of a superior UK court. The principle remains reflected in the Court’s current Article 7 guidance.[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)][[bihr.org](https://bihr.org.uk/get-informed/legislation-explainers/whats-in-the-human-rights-act)]

## Application: How the Principles Work Together

### Hypothetical 1: Immoral but not criminal

A person behaves in a way that attracts widespread condemnation. Before punishment can follow, a court must identify an applicable offence and establish its requirements. Moral disapproval cannot replace the legal basis required by Article 7.[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)]

### Hypothetical 2: A later offence or heavier penalty

Conduct occurs before a new offence is created. The new offence cannot simply be applied retrospectively to that conduct. Likewise, Article 7 prohibits imposing a heavier penalty than the one applicable when the offence occurred.[[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1998/42/schedule/1)]

### Hypothetical 3: An incomplete offence

A person intends to commit an offence but is interrupted. In Northern Ireland, intention alone is insufficient for an attempt: the prosecution must establish an act that is more than merely preparatory, alongside the other statutory requirements.[[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1983/1120)]

## Critical Analysis: Overcriminalisation

Overcriminalisation describes the concern that criminal law extends too widely, criminalising conduct unnecessarily or using criminal punishment where another response would be more appropriate.

Your notes identify three connected concerns:

- Comprehensibility: numerous, overlapping or technical offences can make the law difficult to understand.
- Institutional burden: unnecessary criminalisation can place additional demands on investigators, prosecutors, courts and penal institutions.
- Disproportionality: criminal sanctions and stigma may be excessive for relatively minor wrongdoing.

These are risks to assess, rather than inevitable consequences of every new offence. The key evaluative questions are whether criminalisation serves a sufficiently important purpose, whether the offence is clearly defined, and whether less punitive alternatives could achieve the same objective.

There is also a distinction between two criticisms:


| Criticism | Central concern |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Overcriminalisation | The law criminalises too much or uses criminal sanctions unnecessarily. |
| Breach of legality | Criminal liability or punishment lacks an adequate, accessible and foreseeable legal basis. |


A clearly drafted offence may still be criticised as unnecessary. Conversely, an offence directed at serious wrongdoing may still breach Article 7 if applied through an unforeseeable expansion of liability.[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)]

## Exam and Coursework Guidance

For a problem involving these introductory principles, use the following structure:

1. Identify the jurisdiction and the applicable offence.
2. Locate its statutory or common-law definition.
3. Identify the actus reus, including any circumstances, result and causation requirements.
4. Identify the required mens rea or any strict-liability element.
5. Consider any available defence.
6. Check whether the conduct and penalty were covered by sufficiently accessible and foreseeable law at the relevant time.
7. Distinguish legal liability from the separate policy question of whether criminalisation is justified.

Common mistakes include treating mens rea as intention alone, treating every omission as criminal, treating all defences as justifications, and assuming that every UK jurisdiction uses the same legislation.

## Quick-Revision Summary

- Criminal law defines offences and authorises punishment while constraining the state’s punitive power.
- Actus reus covers the offence’s external elements, not merely a physical act.
- Mens rea concerns the required mental or fault elements, not motive alone.
- Inchoate liability includes offences such as attempt and conspiracy.
- *Mala in se* and *mala prohibita* distinguish perceived inherent wrongfulness from wrongfulness based on legal prohibition.
- Article 7 prohibits retrospective criminalisation and heavier retrospective penalties.
- Foreseeable judicial clarification is permissible; arbitrary expansion of liability is not.
- Article 7(2) is not a general licence to punish conduct retrospectively because it seems immoral.[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)][[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1983/1120)][[getcaselaw](https://www.getcaselaw.com/guides/mens-rea-actus-reus)]

## Self-Test


| Question | Model answer guidance |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Must every criminal offence be statutory? | No. Article 7 recognises both legislation and case law as sources of law. |
| 2. Why is actus reus broader than “the physical act”? | It may also include omissions where a duty exists, circumstances, prohibited results and causation. |
| 3. Is mens rea the same as intention? | No. The offence may require another mental state or fault standard, such as recklessness or negligence. |
| 4. Is intending to commit an offence sufficient for an attempt in Northern Ireland? | No. Article 3 also requires an act that is more than merely preparatory, subject to the statutory framework. |
| 5. Can courts develop criminal law without breaching Article 7? | Yes, where development is consistent with the essence of the offence and reasonably foreseeable; S.W. illustrates this. |
| 6. Does Article 7(2) permit punishment for anything later considered immoral? | No. It refers to conduct already criminal under the relevant general principles when committed. |
| 7. How does overcriminalisation differ from illegality under Article 7? | Overcriminalisation challenges the scope or justification of criminal law; Article 7 concerns the legal basis and predictability of liability and punishment. |


The legal foundations for these answers are the Article 7 case-law guidance, the Northern Ireland attempts legislation and the general elements of criminal liability.[[ks.echr.coe](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/guide_art_7_eng-pdf)][[legislation.gov](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/1983/1120)][[getcaselaw](https://www.getcaselaw.com/guides/mens-rea-actus-reus)]
