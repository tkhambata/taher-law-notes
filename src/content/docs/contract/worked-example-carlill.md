## The question

When can a reward advertisement be a unilateral offer accepted by performing its conditions?

## Case at a glance

- Case: *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company* [1893] 1 QB 256; [1892] EWCA Civ 1.
- Court: Court of Appeal, England and Wales.
- Decision: 7 December 1892.
- Topics: offer, acceptance by performance, consideration and objective seriousness.
- Outcome: the company's appeal was dismissed; Mrs Carlill was entitled to the reward.

## Material facts

The company advertised a £100 reward for someone who contracted influenza after using its smoke ball three times daily for two weeks as directed. It referred to £1,000 deposited with a bank as evidence of sincerity.

Mrs Carlill used the product as directed and nevertheless contracted influenza. She claimed the reward. The company denied that the advertisement created an enforceable contract.

## Issues

1. Was this advertisement a sufficiently definite and serious offer rather than sales puff or an invitation to negotiate?
2. Could an offer addressed to the public become binding through one person's performance?
3. Was separate advance notice of acceptance necessary under this offer?
4. Was there consideration for the promise?

## Holding and reasoning

### Read the particular promise

The court treated this advertisement as a serious promise to pay a defined reward on stated conditions. The reference to the bank deposit supported that reading. The lesson is not that every advertisement is an offer: analyse the actual words and context.

### Identify acceptance by performance

The offer could be addressed to the public and accepted by someone who fulfilled its conditions. On the construction of this offer, separate advance notification was not required; the requested performance supplied acceptance.

Do not turn that reasoning into a general rule that acceptance never needs communication. The nature and terms of the offer matter.

### Analyse consideration separately

The requested use involved inconvenience to the user and commercial benefit to the company through promoting use and sales. Keep consideration separate from the question whether an offer existed. Do not reduce the reasoning to a requirement that the claimant must have bought directly from the company.

## Practice problem - fictional facts

A study app promises £50 to a user who completes its specified 14-day revision programme and still fails a named mock test. It states that a fund has been set aside for eligible claims. Sam reads the promise, completes the programme as specified, and fails the named test. The app refuses to pay, saying it was only advertising.

### Worked answer outline

- Issue: was there a serious unilateral offer, accepted by the required performance?
- Authority: use *Carlill* to support the possibility that a sufficiently definite reward promise can be accepted by performance. Explain why wording and context matter.
- Application: the specified programme, named test and stated fund support seriousness and certainty. Check the full terms and evidence of Sam's compliance. Address consideration separately.
- Provisional conclusion: Sam has a plausible analogy on the stated facts, but the precise terms and evidence remain important. This is practice reasoning, not a guaranteed outcome.

## Common mistakes

- Treating every advertisement as an offer.
- Saying acceptance never needs to be communicated.
- Repeating the facts without explaining the reasoning.
- Treating an English authority as automatically decisive in another jurisdiction.
- Presenting fictional practice facts as a real reported case.
