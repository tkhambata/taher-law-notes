## Overview

Trespass protects bodily integrity, personal liberty and possession of property against unjustified interference. The main forms of trespass to the person are assault, battery and false imprisonment.

These three torts are **actionable per se**: the claimant does not need to prove physical injury or financial loss. However, the claimant must still establish the elements of the tort, and the defendant may establish a lawful justification. Actual injury or loss affects the damages available.


| Tort | What it protects | Essential requirement |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Assault | Freedom from anticipation of immediate unlawful force | Reasonable apprehension of an immediate battery |
| Battery | Bodily integrity | Intentional, direct and legally unjustified physical contact |
| False imprisonment | Freedom of movement | Intentional, total and unlawful restraint |
| Intentional infliction of harm | Physical and psychiatric health | Unjustified conduct, the required intention and resulting physical harm or recognised psychiatric illness |
| Trespass to goods | Possession of movable property | Unauthorised direct interference with goods |
| Trespass to land | Possession of land | Unjustified entry onto or direct interference with land |


The rule in Wilkinson v Downton is related to trespass to the person but is a distinct tort: unlike assault, battery and false imprisonment, it requires actual harm.

## General Principles of Trespass

## Intentional interference and accidental injury

The modern starting point for trespass to the person is intentional interference. An accidental injury caused by carelessness ordinarily belongs in negligence.

An intention to injure is not always necessary. For battery, deliberately making the relevant physical contact may suffice even if the resulting injury was neither intended nor expected.

## Letang v Cooper 1965 1 QB 232

- Facts: The defendant drove over the claimant’s legs while she was sunbathing. She attempted to frame the accidental injury as trespass rather than negligence.
- Principle: Unintentional personal injury caused by carelessness should be pursued in negligence, not relabelled as intentional trespass.
- Why remember it: It separates intentional interference from negligent injury. Start with this case when deciding which cause of action fits an accident.[legalguide](https://legalguide.ie/personal-trespass/2/)

## Directness and intervening reactions

Direct interference does not require the defendant personally to touch the claimant. Force may operate through an object, and an instinctive protective reaction by another person need not break the connection with the defendant’s act.

## Scott v Shepherd (1773) 2 Wm Bl 892

- Facts: The defendant threw a lighted squib into a crowded market. Others threw it onwards to protect themselves before it exploded and injured the claimant.
- Principle: The intervening protective reactions did not prevent the original thrower from being liable in trespass.
- Why remember it: “Direct” does not mean “without any intervening movement”. A defendant cannot necessarily escape responsibility because others instinctively reacted to the danger they created.

## Voluntary conduct and transferred intention

A genuinely involuntary movement is different from a deliberate interference. Equally, a person who intentionally directs force at one individual may remain liable where it strikes someone else: the intended target and the actual victim need not always be the same.

The useful distinction is between intending the interference and intending all its consequences. The latter is not generally necessary for battery.

## Assault

## Definition and requirements

Assault occurs where the defendant intentionally causes the claimant reasonably to apprehend an immediate battery.

Ask:

1. Did the defendant intentionally create the apprehension?
2. Did the claimant anticipate unlawful physical contact?
3. Was that apprehension reasonable?
4. Was the threatened contact sufficiently immediate?

No contact is necessary. “Apprehension” means anticipation, not necessarily fear: a confident person can apprehend a blow without being frightened by it.

## Words and conduct must be assessed together

Words can create a threat, qualify it or remove its apparent immediacy. Mere insults should be distinguished from words conveying an imminent attack.

## Tuberville v Savage (1669) 1 Mod Rep 3

- Facts: The defendant placed his hand on his sword but accompanied the gesture with words indicating that he would not attack because it was assize time.
- Principle: Words can negate an apparent threat by showing that immediate violence is not intended.
- Why remember it: Never assess a threatening gesture in isolation. What the defendant says can turn an apparent assault into a non-assault.[legalguide](https://legalguide.ie/personal-trespass/2/)

## Dullaghan v Hillen and King 1957 Ir Jur Rep 10

- Facts: An encounter with customs officers involved insulting language and physical violence.
- Principle: The judgment expressed the older proposition that words alone could not constitute assault; insults did not themselves justify a violent response.
- Why remember it: It illustrates the distinction between verbal provocation and threatened physical force. For NI/UK law, remember the modern approach in Ireland rather than treating its categorical statement about words as controlling. This is a Republic of Ireland authority.[legalguide](https://legalguide.ie/personal-trespass/2/)

## R v Ireland 1998 AC 147

- Facts: The defendant made repeated silent telephone calls that caused psychiatric injury.
- Principle: Words, and in an appropriate context silence, can amount to assault. The question is whether the communication causes apprehension of sufficiently immediate unlawful violence.
- Why remember it: Assault does not require a raised fist or spoken threat. Context can give silence a threatening meaning. This is a criminal authority relevant to understanding assault, rather than a substitute for analysing the civil tort.[legalguide](https://legalguide.ie/personal-trespass/2/)

## Thomas v National Union of Mineworkers (South Wales Area) 1986 Ch 20

- Facts: Working miners encountered threatening picketers while being transported through police protection.
- Principle: Threatening conduct does not establish assault unless immediate violence can reasonably be apprehended; practical barriers to an attack matter.
- Why remember it: It tests immediacy. Distinguish intimidating behaviour from a threat that could realistically be carried out immediately.

## Relationship with harassment

Conduct falling short of assault may still require analysis under the Protection from Harassment (Northern Ireland) Order 1997. Harassment and assault address different requirements: the absence of an immediate threatened battery does not necessarily make a sustained pattern of intimidation lawful.

## Battery

## Definition and requirements

Battery is the intentional and direct application of physical contact without consent or another lawful justification.

The essential questions are:

1. Was the relevant contact intentional?
2. Was it sufficiently direct?
3. Was it outside ordinary acceptable contact?
4. Was it justified by consent, lawful authority or another defence?

Neither physical injury nor an intention to injure is required. Contact through clothing or through an object can qualify.lawteacher+1

## Cole v Turner (1704) 6 Mod Rep 149

- Context: The judgment distinguishes touching in anger from ordinary, non-violent contact encountered in everyday life.
- Principle: Even slight unlawful touching can amount to battery; substantial force is unnecessary.
- Why remember it: Battery protects bodily autonomy, not merely freedom from serious violence. Its historical language about anger should not be converted into a universal requirement that the defendant must be angry.

## Collins v Wilcock 1984 1 WLR 1172

- Facts: A police officer took hold of a woman’s arm without a sufficient legal basis for restraining her.
- Principle: Physical restraint requires justification. However, ordinary contact generally acceptable in everyday life does not ordinarily constitute battery.
- Why remember it: This is the leading practical illustration of the boundary between everyday touching and unlawful interference. Police status does not itself authorise grabbing someone.[legalguide](https://legalguide.ie/personal-trespass/2/)

## Wilson v Pringle 1987 QB 237

- Facts: A schoolboy pulled another pupil’s bag in a prank, causing injury.
- Principle: The Court of Appeal described actionable touching as “hostile”, distinguishing it from ordinary physical interaction.
- Why remember it: It highlights the importance of context. “Hostility” should not be reduced to personal hatred or an intention to injure; the central concern is whether the interference was legally unjustified.[legalguide](https://legalguide.ie/personal-trespass/2/)

## Lane v Holloway 1968 1 QB 379

- Facts: An altercation culminated in a much younger man striking an older man and causing serious injury.
- Principle: Participation in an altercation does not necessarily amount to consent to seriously disproportionate violence.
- Why remember it: “They agreed to fight” is not a complete answer. Analyse the nature and degree of force, rather than assuming consent covers everything that follows.

## False Imprisonment

## Definition and requirements

False imprisonment is the intentional and unlawful total restraint of another person’s freedom of movement.

It does not require a prison cell. Confinement can result from physical barriers, force or submission to an assertion of authority. However, obstruction, inconvenience and delay are not automatically imprisonment.legalguide+1

## Bird v Jones (1845) 7 QB 742

- Facts: The claimant was prevented from proceeding along part of a bridge but could leave in another direction.
- Principle: Restraint must be total. Preventing movement in one direction is insufficient where a reasonable alternative remains.
- Why remember it: It provides the central distinction between imprisonment and obstruction: inability to take a preferred route is not necessarily loss of liberty.[legalguide](https://legalguide.ie/false-imprisonment-defences/2/)

## Phillips v Great Northern Railway Co Ltd (1903) 4 NIJR 154

- Facts: Railway staff delayed a passenger’s departure during a ticket dispute. She was nevertheless able to leave the station.
- Principle: Delay and interference with an intended means of departure are insufficient without total restraint. Psychological domination can matter, but it must actually deprive the claimant of liberty.
- Why remember it: An authoritative tone or instruction to wait does not automatically establish imprisonment. Ask whether the person was genuinely confined, rather than merely delayed or challenged.[legalguide](https://legalguide.ie/false-imprisonment-defences/2/)

## Intention, omissions and duties to release

A deliberate act affecting movement is not necessarily enough. The defendant’s responsibility for bringing about confinement and the relevant mental element must be examined.

A failure to release someone can be actionable where a duty to release exists, but not every omission creating inconvenience is false imprisonment.

## Iqbal v Prison Officers Association 2009 EWCA Civ 1312; 2010 QB 732

- Facts: A prison officers’ strike resulted in the claimant remaining in his cell instead of spending his usual time outside it.
- Principle: The union was not liable merely because the strike resulted in continued confinement. False imprisonment requires the necessary responsibility for, and mental connection to, the restraint; omissions generally require a specific duty to act.
- Why remember it: Foreseeable confinement following someone’s inaction is not automatically intentional false imprisonment. Examine who imposed the restraint, what they intended or subjectively foresaw, and whether they had a duty to release.

## Burns v Johnston 1916 2 IR 444; affirmed 1917 2 IR 137

- Facts: Factory workers demanded to leave before the end of working hours as extended under arrangements the court treated as accepted. The employer maintained the ordinary locked-gate and pass system.
- Principle: On those facts, the employer’s failure to provide early departure facilities was not false imprisonment. The court distinguished this from actively detaining a worker or wrongfully refusing a release to which they were entitled.
- Why remember it: It is a case about consent, existing arrangements and the limits of liability for omissions—not a general authority permitting employers to imprison workers.[legalguide](https://legalguide.ie/false-imprisonment-defences/2/)

## Louis v Commonwealth (1987) 87 FLR 277

- Context: This Australian decision is discussed in false-imprisonment authorities concerning confinement and damages.
- Principle: False imprisonment concerns confinement within a bounded area; restrictions on leaving a whole country are not necessarily imprisonment in this sense. Damages also depend on the claimant’s individual experience of the detention.
- Why remember it: Not every restriction on travel is false imprisonment. Identify the actual area of confinement and distinguish restrictions on travel from total personal restraint. It is comparative Australian [authority.robinson.com](http://authority.robinson.com)

## Awareness and reasonable escape

The claimant need not necessarily know about confinement while it occurs, although awareness and distress may affect damages.

An apparent escape route must also be reasonably usable. The law does not ordinarily expect a claimant to escape by risking injury or submitting to unreasonable humiliation.

## Austin v Commissioner of Police of the Metropolis 2009 UKHL 5

- Facts: Police contained a crowd at Oxford Circus during a volatile demonstration, restricting demonstrators and bystanders for several hours.
- Principle: The House of Lords held that Article 5 was not engaged on the particular facts. The context and operation of the containment mattered to whether it amounted to a deprivation of liberty.
- Why remember it: Common-law false imprisonment and an Article 5 deprivation of liberty are distinct questions. The House of Lords appeal concerned Article 5, not a general defence permitting all crowd containment.[hudoc.echr.coe](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=001-109581)

## Austin and Others v United Kingdom 2012 ECHR 459

- Facts: The applicants challenged the same police containment before the European Court of Human Rights.
- Principle: On the specific and exceptional facts, containment that was unavoidable, necessary to avert a real risk of serious injury or damage, and kept to the minimum required did not amount to an Article 5 deprivation of liberty.
- Why remember it: The decision is context-sensitive, not blanket approval of “kettling”. Continued necessity and the way the measure operates are crucial.[hudoc.echr.coe](https://hudoc.echr.coe.int/eng?i=002-64)

## R (Lumba) v Secretary of State for the Home Department 2011 UKSC 12

- Facts: Foreign national prisoners were detained under an unpublished policy inconsistent with the published detention policy.
- Principle: The detention was unlawful despite the existence of statutory detention powers. However, only nominal damages were available where lawful application of the proper policy would have produced the same detention.
- Why remember it: Separate two questions: “Was the detention lawful?” and “What loss did the illegality cause?” A claimant can establish false imprisonment without obtaining substantial compensation.blackstonechambers+1

## Intentional Infliction of Physical or Psychiatric Harm

## Nature of the tort

This tort concerns unjustified words or conduct intended to cause physical harm or severe mental or emotional distress, resulting in physical harm or recognised psychiatric illness.

It should not be confused with a general right not to be upset, offended or distressed.supremecourt+1

## Wilkinson v Downton 1897 2 QB 57

- Facts: As a supposed practical joke, the defendant falsely told the claimant that her husband had suffered a serious accident. She suffered serious consequences.
- Principle: Deliberately inflicted harm can be actionable without physical contact or an immediate threatened battery.
- Why remember it: It fills a gap left by assault and battery: words can intentionally cause serious harm even where no one touches or immediately threatens the claimant.

## Powell v Boladz 1998 Lloyd’s Rep Med 116

- Facts: Parents alleged that doctors had falsified or substituted medical records following their child’s death and that the deception caused psychiatric harm.
- Principle: Deliberate deception does not, without the necessary intention to cause injury, establish liability under the Wilkinson principle. The claim failed to satisfy the required basis for liability.
- Why remember it: Intending to conceal information is not necessarily intending to cause psychiatric harm. Identify the precise intention rather than treating dishonesty itself as sufficient.i-law+1

## Breslin v McKenna 2009 NIQB 50

- Facts: Victims and relatives brought civil proceedings arising from the Omagh bombing, relying on battery, intentional infliction of harm and conspiracy to injure.
- Principle: Serious terrorist violence can be analysed through ordinary civil causes of action. The proceedings applied the civil standard of proof, notwithstanding the gravity of the allegations, and treated the explosion as capable of directly inflicting injury.
- Why remember it: It connects the lecture’s abstract principles to a major Northern Ireland case. Civil liability is distinct from criminal conviction, and direct force need not be applied by hand.[casemine](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/uk/5a8ff7ee60d03e7f57eb306d)

## Rhodes v OPO 2015 UKSC 32

- Facts: Proceedings sought to prevent publication of a father’s autobiographical account of childhood abuse because of the feared psychiatric effect on his son.
- Principle: The tort requires unjustified words or conduct directed towards the claimant, an actual intention to cause physical harm or severe mental or emotional distress, and resulting physical harm or recognised psychiatric illness. Recklessness is insufficient.
- Why remember it: This is the modern controlling framework for the Wilkinson tort. Foreseeability of distress cannot replace actual intention, and distress alone cannot replace the required harm.supremecourt+1

## The three-part modern test

1. Conduct: Words or conduct directed towards the claimant without justification or reasonable excuse.
2. Intention: An intention to cause physical harm or severe mental or emotional distress.
3. Consequence: Physical harm or a recognised psychiatric illness.

The mental element and consequence element are different: the defendant need not intend a particular psychiatric diagnosis, but the claimant must suffer the legally required harm.supremecourt+1

## Trespass to Goods and Digital Interference

## General principle

Trespass to goods concerns direct interference with movable property in another person’s possession without lawful authority. The lecture’s digital examples are United States authorities and should not be treated as establishing the NI/UK position.

## Thrifty-Tel Inc v Bezenek, 46 Cal App 4th 1559 (1996)

- Facts: Unauthorised use of a telephone system overburdened the network and denied some customers access.
- Principle: Electronic interference with a system could support trespass-to-chattels liability where it impaired the system’s use.
- Why remember it: Physical damage is not the only relevant interference: impairment of functioning can matter. This is a Californian application of the [tort.law.justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2003/s103781a.html)

## CompuServe Inc v Cyber Promotions Inc, 962 F Supp 1015 (SD Ohio 1997)

- Facts: A bulk-email business continued sending unsolicited messages through CompuServe’s systems despite objections, consuming resources and affecting the service.
- Principle: Substantial unauthorised electronic interference impairing computer resources could support an injunction based on trespass to personal property.
- Why remember it: It explains why the lecture mentions spam: the claim concerned interference with the system, not simply annoyance at receiving unwanted [messages.law.justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2003/s103781a.html)

## Intel Corp v Hamidi, 30 Cal 4th 1342 (2003)

- Facts: A former employee sent emails criticising Intel to its employees, but the messages did not damage or impair the computer system.
- Principle: Under California law, electronic communications that neither damage the recipient’s system nor impair its functioning do not establish this form of trespass to chattels.
- Why remember it: It supplies the limit to the earlier cases. Unwanted communication is not automatically actionable interference with computer [property.law.justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2003/s103781a.html)

Academic commentary on Hamidi debates whether requiring harm to the computer system protects communication appropriately or underprotects owners against unauthorised use of their resources. This illustrates the tension between property control and freedom of communication; it does not establish a corresponding NI/UK cause of [action.repository.law](http://action.repository.law).

## Consent

## General requirements

Consent may be express or inferred from conduct. It must be valid, freely given and sufficiently connected to the act performed.

Consider capacity, pressure, deception, the scope of permission and any withdrawal of consent. The maxim volenti non fit injuria expresses the broader idea that a person cannot ordinarily complain of an interference to which they validly agreed.

## O’Brien v Cunard Steamship Co, 28 NE 266 (Mass 1891)

- Facts: A passenger presented her arm for vaccination in circumstances indicating agreement.
- Principle: Consent can be inferred from outward conduct reasonably understood as agreement.
- Why remember it: Consent does not always require spoken words. The legal significance of outward conduct may differ from an undisclosed private intention. ## Gillick v West Norfolk and Wisbech Area Health Authority 1986 AC 112

- Facts: The case concerned advice and contraceptive treatment for girls under 16 without parental knowledge or consent.
- Principle: A child under 16 may consent where they have sufficient understanding and intelligence to understand the proposed intervention.
- Why remember it: Capacity is decision-specific, not determined exclusively by age. Assess the child’s understanding of the particular treatment and its implications.

## F v West Berkshire Health Authority 1990 2 AC 1

- Facts: The case concerned proposed sterilisation of an adult woman unable to give valid consent.
- Principle: At common law, necessary treatment of an adult unable to consent may be justified where it is in that person’s best interests.
- Why remember it: Absence of consent does not automatically prohibit all treatment, but incapacity is not permission to do whatever others prefer. Necessity and best interests provide the justification, not fictional

For Northern Ireland, the Mental Capacity Act (Northern Ireland) 2016 has been partially commenced. Ordinary treatment decisions must be analysed under the applicable combination of common law and legislation, rather than automatically importing the Mental Capacity Act 2005 framework from England and [Wales.health-ni.gov](http://Wales.health-ni.gov)+1

## R v Williams 1923 1 KB 340

- Facts: A singing teacher induced a pupil to submit to sexual intercourse by representing it as a procedure to improve her voice.
- Principle: Deception about the nature of the act can prevent apparent agreement from constituting valid consent.
- Why remember it: Ask what the person actually understood themselves to be agreeing to. This criminal authority illustrates the distinction between agreement to the act and apparent agreement produced by fundamental deception.

## Papadimitropoulos v R (1957) 98 CLR 249

- Facts: A woman agreed to intercourse believing that she and the defendant were validly married, following a purported marriage ceremony.
- Principle: The Australian court distinguished deception concerning marital status from deception about the physical nature of intercourse.
- Why remember it: Not every deception has historically invalidated consent in the same way. The case illustrates the difficult boundary between the nature of an act and surrounding circumstances; it is not a modern universal rule about sexual consent.[cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/legal-studies/article/abs/failure-of-medical-advice-trespass-or-negligence/DA6240FC30795A0EF1C7E73C108F23B7)

## Norberg v Wynrib 1992 2 SCR 226

- Facts: A doctor exploited a patient’s drug dependency by supplying medication in exchange for sexual acts.
- Principle: Exploitation and a substantial imbalance of power can undermine purported consent. The Supreme Court of Canada allowed the patient’s appeal, although the judges differed in their legal reasoning.
- Why remember it: Apparent agreement is not necessarily free agreement. Dependency and exploitation matter, particularly within a professional relationship. This is Canadian comparative authority.canlii+2

## Reibl v Hughes 1980 2 SCR 880

- Facts: A patient agreed to surgery but alleged inadequate disclosure of its risks after suffering a serious adverse outcome.
- Principle: Inadequate risk disclosure ordinarily raises negligence rather than battery where the patient agreed to the nature of the procedure performed.
- Why remember it: Distinguish “I did not agree to that procedure” from “I agreed, but was not properly warned”. ## Secretary of State for the Home Department v Robb 1995 Fam 127

- Facts: A prisoner on hunger strike refused food and medical intervention, despite understanding the potentially fatal consequences.
- Principle: A competent adult’s effective refusal must be respected, even where it risks death.
- Why remember it: Necessity is not simply “saving life at any cost”. A protective motive does not automatically override bodily autonomy.

## Marshall v Curry 1933 3 DLR 260

- Facts: During a hernia operation, the surgeon removed a diseased testicle without obtaining separate consent.
- Principle: An unforeseen and sufficiently urgent surgical necessity may justify extending an operation when consent cannot practicably be obtained.
- Why remember it: It addresses emergency expansion of treatment, not a general permission to perform additional procedures merely because they seem beneficial. This is Canadian comparative authority.publications+1

## Re A (Children) (Conjoined Twins: Surgical Separation) 2001 Fam 147

- Facts: Separation would save one twin but inevitably cause the other’s death; without separation, both were expected to die.
- Principle: The Court of Appeal permitted separation in exceptional circumstances, with the judges adopting differing legal reasoning, including necessity.
- Why remember it: It demonstrates how demanding and exceptional necessity can be where fundamental rights conflict. It is not authority for a general rule that one life may be sacrificed whenever more lives can be saved.

## Lawful authority

A statutory power can justify contact or detention only where its conditions are satisfied and it is lawfully exercised.

Collins v Wilcock demonstrates that an officer needs a legal basis for physical restraint. Lumba demonstrates that the existence of a general detention power does not cure an unlawful detention decision.legalguide+1

## Applying the Law

Use the following sequence when analysing an incident:

1. Identify the protected interest: bodily integrity, apprehension of force, freedom of movement or possession.
2. Identify each possible tort separately; one incident may involve several.
3. Establish the required act and mental element.
4. Test the distinctive requirement: immediacy for assault, contact for battery, total restraint for false imprisonment.
5. Consider consent and other lawful justifications.
6. Separate liability from compensation.
7. For intentionally inflicted psychiatric harm without contact or confinement, apply the Rhodes framework separately.
